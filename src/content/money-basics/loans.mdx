## What it is

A loan is simply someone else's money, borrowed against a promise to repay it with interest, over a fixed schedule. That's the whole concept. Everything else — the paperwork, the collateral, the credit check — exists to answer one question for the lender: how likely are you to keep that promise?

## How it actually works in India

Every loan you take is priced against your creditworthiness, which in India is summarised by a credit score maintained by one of a small number of licensed credit information companies — TransUnion CIBIL is the most commonly referenced, alongside Experian, Equifax, and CRIF High Mark. Lenders (banks and NBFCs, both regulated by the Reserve Bank of India) pull this score to decide not just whether to lend to you, but at what interest rate.

Loans split into two broad categories that matter more than most people realise:

**Secured loans** — home loans, loan against property, gold loans — are backed by an asset the lender can claim if you default. Because the lender's risk is lower, the interest rate is almost always lower too.

**Unsecured loans** — personal loans, most credit card debt — have nothing backing them except your promise and your credit history. The lender's risk is higher, so the rate is higher, often substantially.

Most loans in India are structured as EMIs (Equated Monthly Instalments) on a reducing-balance basis: each instalment is a fixed total amount, but the split between interest and principal shifts over the loan's life. Early instalments are mostly interest; later ones are mostly principal. This isn't a trick — it's just the mathematically correct way to charge interest on a shrinking balance — but it does mean prepaying early in a loan's life saves you far more interest than prepaying the same amount late in its life.

<Callout>
The reducing-balance structure is exactly why two loans with the "same" interest rate and tenure can have very different total costs if one lets you prepay without penalty and the other doesn't. The rate on the paper isn't the whole story.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Comparing only the EMI, not the total interest.** A longer tenure lowers your monthly EMI and makes a loan feel more affordable — while quietly increasing the total interest you'll pay over the life of the loan. The EMI answers "can I afford this month." It doesn't answer "what is this actually costing me."

**Treating a lower advertised rate as the whole picture.** Processing fees, prepayment penalties, and mandatory insurance add-ons change the effective cost of a loan without changing the headline rate. The number that matters is the total cost across the loan's life, not the rate quoted in the ad.

**Not checking whether prepayment makes mathematical sense before doing it anyway.** Prepaying a loan is emotionally satisfying — debt-free feels good — but if the loan's interest rate is meaningfully below what you could reasonably expect to earn by investing that same money instead, prepaying isn't the financially optimal move, even though it's often framed as the responsible one.

## A worked example

Consider two loans of the same amount and the same tenure, where one has a materially lower interest rate than the other because it's secured against an asset (say, a home loan) versus unsecured (a personal loan). Even a few percentage points of rate difference compounds, across a multi-year tenure, into a total interest gap that's often larger than people expect when they only compared the monthly EMI. This is the actual argument for using a secured loan for a large purchase where one is available, rather than defaulting to an unsecured one for convenience: the gap isn't in the monthly affordability, it's in the total cost.

The precise rate you'd be offered depends on your credit score, income, and the lender's current pricing — figures that change often enough that quoting a specific percentage here would be stale by the time you read it. Check your actual offered rate against your credit report before assuming any number, including ones you've seen quoted elsewhere.
