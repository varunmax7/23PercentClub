## What it is

Tax isn't a separate topic from investing — it's a variable inside every investing decision, because the return you actually keep is your return after tax, not the number quoted before it. Two instruments with identical pre-tax returns can leave you with meaningfully different amounts depending on how each is taxed.

## How it actually works in India

India's income tax system gives salaried individuals a choice between two regimes each year: one with more deductions and exemptions available but higher slab rates, and a newer one with fewer deductions but lower slab rates. Which one results in less tax depends entirely on your specific income and how many deductions you actually use — there's no single right answer, and the better choice can change from year to year as your situation changes.

Separately from the regime choice, common tax-advantaged instruments — Provident Fund contributions, ELSS mutual funds, life insurance premiums, and others — fall under specific sections of the Income Tax Act that allow deductions on the amount invested, up to limits that are periodically revised in the Union Budget. Because these limits change, and because this page can't stay current with every Budget the way a live government source can, check the Income Tax Department's own site for the figure that applies to the year you're filing for, rather than trusting a number quoted anywhere else, including here.

<Callout>
One change worth knowing specifically, because it's recent enough that many investors still don't: debt mutual fund units acquired on or after 1 April 2023 lost the long-term capital gains treatment (with indexation) that debt funds used to get. Gains on those units are now taxed at your slab rate regardless of how long you hold them, under the provisions introduced via Section 50AA. This changed the comparison between debt funds and other fixed-income instruments materially for anyone investing after that date.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Choosing an investment purely for its tax deduction, without checking whether it's actually a good fit for the goal.** A deduction reduces your taxable income; it doesn't automatically make the underlying instrument a good long-term holding. Tax efficiency is one input into a decision, not the whole decision.

**Not re-evaluating the old-versus-new regime choice every year.** Your optimal regime depends on your income and deductions in that specific year. Sticking with whichever you picked once, without recalculating, can quietly cost you money as your financial situation changes.

**Assuming debt mutual funds are still taxed the way they were before April 2023.** This is common enough, and consequential enough, that it's the single most important tax fact on this page — see the callout above.

## A worked example

Consider an ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme) mutual fund SIP versus a comparable non-tax-advantaged equity fund SIP, with an identical assumed pre-tax return. The ELSS investment also qualifies for a deduction on the amount invested each year under Section 80C (subject to the current limit and to the overall 80C ceiling being shared across all your 80C instruments, not just this one), which lowers your taxable income for that year in a way the non-ELSS fund does not. Whether that deduction is worth more to you than the ELSS fund's mandatory lock-in period costs you in flexibility depends on your tax bracket and how soon you might need the money — which is a genuinely individual calculation, not a rule that applies the same way to everyone.
