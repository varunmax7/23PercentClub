## What it is

A credit card is a revolving line of unsecured credit: the bank extends you a spending limit, you spend against it, and once a month you're billed for what you spent. Pay the full bill by the due date and you've effectively borrowed money for free for the length of that billing cycle. Pay less than the full bill, and the remaining balance starts accruing interest — usually at a rate far higher than almost any other consumer loan.

## How it actually works in India

Every card has a billing cycle (typically around a month) and a due date shortly after the cycle closes — the gap between the two is the interest-free period, but only if you're paying your bill in full. The moment you carry even a small balance past the due date, that interest-free grace period disappears not just for the new balance, but often for new spending too, depending on the card's terms.

Card issuers are required to show a "minimum amount due" on every bill — usually a small fraction of the total outstanding. Paying only that minimum keeps your account in good standing and avoids a late-payment mark, but the remaining balance keeps accruing interest, and that interest compounds. This is structurally the same mechanism that makes a SIP grow — money growing on itself over time — except here it's working against you instead of for you.

<Callout>
Credit card interest rates are among the highest most people will ever encounter in consumer lending. Whatever specific annual rate your card charges, it is very likely far above what any reasonably diversified long-term investment could be expected to earn — which is the entire argument for treating "pay the statement in full" as close to a non-negotiable rule, not a nice-to-have.
</Callout>

Your credit utilisation — how much of your total available limit you're using — also feeds into your credit score, maintained by bureaus like TransUnion CIBIL. Running a card close to its limit, even if you pay it off every month, can still work against your score, because utilisation is measured at the point your statement is generated, not after you've paid it.

## Common mistakes

**Treating the minimum due as a real payment option rather than an emergency fallback.** It's marketed as a convenience. Structurally, it's a way to keep a high-interest balance revolving indefinitely while feeling like you're "handling" it.

**Closing old cards to "simplify," without checking the effect on utilisation and credit history length.** Both your total available credit and the age of your oldest account factor into your credit profile. Closing a long-held, no-fee card can quietly lower your score even if the card itself was never the problem.

**Not distinguishing between a card used as a payment tool and a card used as a lending source.** Using a card for the float and rewards, while paying it off in full every cycle, is a genuinely different financial behaviour than using it to extend spending you couldn't otherwise afford. Both look identical on the statement. They are not identical in what they cost you.

## A worked example

The mechanism that makes revolving credit card debt expensive is the same compounding mechanism the SIP calculator in this site's Tools section illustrates — the sign is just flipped. A SIP compounds a contribution you keep adding, in your favour. A revolving credit card balance compounds an amount you're not fully repaying, against you. Run any of this site's growth calculators with a high enough assumed annual rate and a multi-year horizon, and the shape of the curve you'll see is the same shape a long-carried credit card balance follows — just working in the opposite direction for your net worth.
