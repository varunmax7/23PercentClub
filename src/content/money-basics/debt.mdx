## What it is

Debt is money you owe. That's the definition, and it's also the reason "debt" alone isn't a useful category to make decisions about — a home loan and a revolving credit card balance are both debt, and they are not remotely the same financial situation.

## How it actually works in India

The distinction that actually matters is usually described as good debt versus bad debt, and it's less about the size of the debt and more about what it did for you and what it costs you.

**Debt taken for an appreciating or income-generating purpose** — a home loan on a property you'll live in or that could appreciate, an education loan that increases your earning capacity, a business loan against a plan with a real return — tends to carry a lower interest rate (often secured against an asset) and moves you toward something.

**Debt taken to fund consumption** — credit card balances, most personal loans, buy-now-pay-later on things that lose value the moment you own them — tends to carry a much higher interest rate and leaves you with nothing to show for it once it's repaid except the repayment itself.

The same rupee amount of debt can be a reasonable financial tool or a genuine problem, depending entirely on which of these two categories it falls into. Most "debt-free" advice treats all debt identically, which is why it can lead people to aggressively prepay a low-rate home loan while a high-rate credit card balance keeps quietly compounding in the background.

<Callout>
A debt-to-income view is more useful than a total-debt-in-rupees view. What matters isn't how large the number is in isolation — it's how much of your monthly income is committed to servicing it, and at what interest rate, relative to what that income could otherwise do.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Paying off debts in the wrong order.** The mathematically optimal order is highest interest rate first, regardless of balance size — this is usually called the "avalanche" method. Many people instead pay off the smallest balance first for the psychological win (the "snowball" method), which can work if it's the only thing that keeps you consistent, but it costs more in total interest than the avalanche approach.

**Conflating "debt-free" with "financially healthy."** Someone with a low-rate home loan and a fully funded emergency fund can be in a stronger financial position than someone with zero debt and no savings. The goal isn't the absence of debt — it's debt that's appropriately priced, sized, and serving a purpose.

**Not accounting for the psychological cost, only the mathematical one.** The avalanche method is mathematically optimal, but if carrying multiple open balances genuinely erodes your ability to stay disciplined, the "wrong" method that you actually stick with can outperform the "right" one you abandon after two months.

## A worked example

Picture two people, each with the same total amount of debt: one entirely in a home loan, the other entirely in credit card balances. The home loan borrower's interest rate is materially lower — often less than half — of what the credit card borrower is paying, purely because the debt is secured and the lender's risk is lower. Over the same repayment period, the credit card borrower pays substantially more in total interest for holding the identical principal amount, not because they borrowed more, but because of what they borrowed it as.

This is the actual argument for consolidating high-interest unsecured debt into a lower-rate secured option where one is genuinely available (not as a way to spend more, but to stop paying the unsecured-debt premium) — and for being far more cautious about taking on debt in the "bad" category in the first place, since its cost compounds faster than most people's intuition expects.
