## What it is

Insurance is a mechanism for transferring risk you can't afford to bear yourself onto an insurer, in exchange for a premium. That's it. It isn't a savings mechanism, a tax trick, or an investment — those are different problems with different tools, even though a large share of what's sold as "insurance" in India tries to bundle them together.

## How it actually works in India

Life insurance in India splits, structurally, into two very different categories that get marketed as if they're variations on the same product.

**Term insurance** is pure risk transfer: you pay a premium, and if you die during the policy term, your nominee receives the sum assured. If you don't, you get nothing back — the same way you don't get your car insurance premium back for not crashing your car. This is the "insurance" part of insurance, and for the amount of coverage it provides, it's the cheapest way to get it.

**Investment-linked plans** — ULIPs (Unit Linked Insurance Plans), endowment plans, money-back policies — bundle a much smaller amount of life cover with an investment or savings component, wrapped into one premium. The insurance portion is typically far smaller than an equivalent term plan would provide for a similar premium, and the investment portion usually underperforms a comparable standalone investment once you account for the charges embedded in the product.

<Callout>
The IRDAI (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India) regulates both categories, but regulation doesn't make the two products equivalent in what they're for. A bundled product isn't "insurance plus investing" in any efficient sense — it's usually a smaller amount of each, sold as one thing, at a combined cost that's harder to see clearly than two separate, simpler products would be.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

**Buying an investment-linked plan because it "feels like" you're not wasting the premium.** Term insurance premiums are only "wasted" in the same sense that any insurance premium is wasted when the covered event doesn't happen — which is the entire point of insurance. Framing term insurance as money lost misunderstands what you bought.

**Sizing life cover based on the premium you're comfortable paying, rather than what your dependents would actually need.** The right amount of cover is a function of your income, your debts, and how many years your dependents would need support — not a round number that felt reasonable when you signed up.

**Treating health insurance as optional if your employer provides a policy.** Employer-provided health cover typically ends when your employment does, often at exactly the moment — a job change, a health event — when you'd want continuous coverage the most. An independent policy, even a modest one, closes that gap.

## A worked example

A sum assured decided on today doesn't stay adequate forever, for the same reason a fixed deposit's nominal value doesn't tell you its real value years from now: inflation erodes what any fixed rupee figure can actually replace. A term cover amount that comfortably supports a family's expenses today can fall well short of that same job a decade or two later, purely because the cost of the things it needs to cover has risen while the sum assured stayed fixed. This is a real argument for reviewing your cover periodically rather than treating the number you picked at 25 as correct for the rest of your life — it's the same erosion this site's Inflation Calculator makes visible for savings, just applied to a coverage amount instead of a deposit.
